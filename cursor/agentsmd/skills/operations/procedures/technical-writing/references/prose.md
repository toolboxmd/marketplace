# Edit for meaning and reader effort

Lead with the useful claim or action. Name the actor, use active verbs, and keep
one main idea per sentence. Keep modifiers beside the words they modify. Replace
ambiguous pronouns and long noun strings with concrete language. Use one term
per concept and preserve exact API names, symbols, units, and quoted errors.

Cut repeated conclusions, vague attribution, generic praise, stock transitions,
and decorative contrasts. Replace a feeling with its mechanism or consequence:
"the workflow is seamless" gives less information than "the saved command opens
the same workspace and restores the last selected dataset."

Prefer familiar words, but do not replace a precise domain term with a loose
synonym. Keep articles, verbs, and connective words when they prevent rereading.
Sentence-length targets are diagnostics, not reasons to produce broken grammar.
Lists belong to genuinely parallel points; do not force every idea into threes.

Preserve confidence and exceptions. "The trace is consistent with contention"
must not become "contention caused the delay" merely to sound direct. Replace
empty hedging with the actual limit or evidence. Do not turn an unsupported
claim into a fact by removing "may."

When simplifying, check that the new wording still answers who, what, when,
under which condition, and with what result. A warning without its trigger can
become misleading; a shortened instruction can accidentally broaden authority.
Keep necessary reasoning beside the choice it informs.

Use formatting to reduce search cost: headings for different reader tasks,
tables for comparisons, and examples for difficult distinctions. Do not add
visuals, metaphors, or large glossaries when a direct sentence is clearer.
